Day01

Meet the Fractions

What they are, what their parts are called, and the four big things they can mean

Fractions look simple at first. Half a pizza. One fourth of a candy bar. Three quarters of an hour. Easy. But the moment math starts asking you to subtract two and one third from one and five sixths, fractions can feel like a secret code.

They are not a secret code. They are a system. Once you understand what the pieces mean, the rules stop feeling like tricks.

The two parts of every fraction

A fraction has two numbers, stacked. The top one is the numerator, and the bottom one is the denominator.

34

The denominator tells you how many equal pieces the whole has been split into. The numerator tells you how many of those pieces you have. So three fourths means the whole was split into 4 equal parts and you have 3 of them.

Three of the four equal pieces are filled in.

Bigger denominator does not mean bigger fraction

Here is the trap: a bigger denominator often makes each piece smaller, because you are cutting the whole into more parts. One half is bigger than one eighth, even though 8 is bigger than 2.

Same bar, two ways to slice it. The half slice is much bigger than the eighth slice.

Pro TipThink of the denominator as the "piece size controller." The bigger it gets (with a numerator of 1), the smaller each individual piece becomes.

Four things a fraction can mean

A fraction can mean part of a whole, like 2 slices of a 6 slice chocolate bar. It can mean part of a group, like 8 out of 20 students wearing sneakers. It can be a number on a number line, sitting between whole numbers. And it can mean division: the bar between the numerator and denominator is a division sign in disguise.

34  =  3 ÷ 4

That last one is huge. If 3 sandwiches are shared equally by 4 people, each person gets three fourths of a sandwich. The fraction is the answer.

Which is larger: one third or one sixth?

If you split a brownie into 3 pieces or 6 pieces, which piece would you rather have?

Day02

The Fraction Family

Proper fractions, improper fractions, and mixed numbers

Fractions come in different styles, and once you can tell them apart you stop being surprised when a problem hands you something like 11/4 or 2 and one third.

Proper fractions

A proper fraction has a numerator smaller than the denominator. That means it is less than 1. Half a sandwich. Three fifths of a pizza. Seven twelfths of an hour. These describe part of one whole.

12  35  712

Improper fractions

An improper fraction has a numerator that is equal to or greater than the denominator. That means it is 1 or more. Five fourths, for example, is more than 1 whole, because 4 fourths already make a whole.

Four fourths fill one whole circle. Plus one more fourth equals five fourths.

Mixed numbers

A mixed number has a whole number paired with a fraction. Two and one half. Three and three fourths. They mean the same thing as improper fractions, but they are easier to picture.

Improper to mixed

Divide the numerator by the denominator. The whole number part is the answer to the division. The remainder becomes the new numerator. The denominator stays the same.

114 →  11 ÷ 4 = 2 R 3  →  234

Mixed to improper

Multiply the whole number by the denominator. Add the numerator. Keep the denominator. So 3 and two fifths becomes (3 × 5 + 2) over 5, which is 17/5.

325 →  (3 × 5) + 2 = 17  →  175
Pro TipThe denominator never changes when you go between improper and mixed. Only the numerator and the whole number move around.

Day03

Twins, Triplets, and Lowest Terms

Equivalent fractions, simplifying, and the GCF

Two fractions can look totally different and still mean the same amount. That is the deal with equivalent fractions. They are twins, triplets, even quintuplets, all standing in for the same value with different outfits on.

12 = 24 = 36 = 48

Making equivalent fractions

Multiply both the numerator and the denominator by the same number. That works because you are multiplying by a sneaky version of 1. Multiplying by four fourths, for example, is just multiplying by 1, which does not change the value.

23 × 44 = 812

Simplifying

Going the other direction is called simplifying. Divide the numerator and the denominator by the same number until you cannot anymore.

The GCF shortcut

The greatest common factor (or GCF) is the biggest number that divides evenly into both the top and the bottom. If you find the GCF first, you can simplify in a single step.

1824 ÷ 66 = 34
Pro TipIf both the numerator and denominator are even, you can always start by dividing by 2. Keep going until you cannot.

Quick reflection

Why does multiplying the top and the bottom by the same number NOT change the value of the fraction?

Day04

Same Size Pieces

Adding and subtracting fractions with like denominators

When two fractions have the same denominator, they are made of pieces the same size. That makes them easy to combine. You just count the pieces.

14 + 24 = 34

One fourth plus two fourths is three fourths. That is just 1 piece plus 2 pieces of the same size. The denominator stays put, because the size of the pieces is not changing.

1/4 (one shaded piece)

1/4 + 2/4 = 3/4 (three shaded pieces total)

The three-step recipe

For adding or subtracting fractions with like denominators: (1) add or subtract the numerators, (2) keep the denominator, and (3) simplify if you can.

29 + 49 = 69 = 23

Six ninths simplifies to two thirds, because 6 and 9 both divide by 3.

Subtraction is the same idea

Seven tenths minus three tenths is just 7 pieces minus 3 pieces of the same size. That leaves 4 pieces. So the answer is four tenths, which simplifies to two fifths.

710 − 310 = 410 = 25
Trap warningNever add the denominators when adding like fractions. 1/4 + 2/4 is NOT 3/8. The pieces are not getting smaller, you are just collecting more of them.

Day05

Different Size Pieces

Adding and subtracting fractions with unlike denominators

Here is where fractions get a reputation. If the denominators are different, you cannot just add the numerators, because the pieces are different sizes. Half a pizza plus a third of a pizza is not 2/5 of a pizza. That answer would actually be smaller than the half you started with, which makes no sense.

Find a common denominator

A common denominator is a number that both denominators can fit into evenly. For 2 and 3, a common denominator is 6, because 2 × 3 = 6 and both 2 and 3 divide into 6. For 4 and 6, a common denominator is 12.

Once you have a common denominator, rename both fractions so they use it. Then add or subtract like you did yesterday, when the pieces matched.

12 + 13 = 36 + 26 = 56

A reliable shortcut

If you cannot think of a common denominator quickly, just multiply the two denominators together. That always gives you one that works, even if it is not the smallest. You can simplify at the end.

23 + 14 = 812 + 312 = 1112

Subtraction works the same

Rename first, then subtract. The hardest part is just remembering to rename before you do anything else.

34 − 16 = 912 − 212 = 712
Sanity checkHalf plus a third should be more than a half. So the answer 5/6 makes sense, because 5/6 is bigger than 1/2. If your answer is somehow smaller than what you started with, something went wrong.

Think it through

Why exactly does 1/2 + 1/3 not equal 2/5? Explain it in your own words, using the idea of piece size.

Day06

Mixed Number Magic

Adding, subtracting, and the dreaded borrowing

Mixed numbers are how real life talks. Two and a half cups of flour. Five and three quarters feet of board. One and a third hours of video. Once you can add and subtract them cleanly, fraction word problems get a lot less scary.

Adding mixed numbers

Add the whole numbers. Add the fractions (rename them first if the denominators are different). Combine.

112 + 213 = 356

Sometimes the fraction part adds up to more than 1. Like 3/4 + 1/2 = 5/4 = 1 and 1/4. When that happens, add the extra whole to your running whole number total.

Subtracting mixed numbers

Subtract whole from whole, fraction from fraction. Easy when the top fraction is bigger than the bottom fraction.

Borrowing (yes, like with regular subtraction)

When the top fraction is smaller than the bottom one, you have to borrow 1 whole and turn it into a fraction first. One whole is the same as denominator-over-denominator. So 1 = 4/4 = 6/6 = 8/8 and so on.

514 → 454

You took 1 from the 5 (giving you 4) and turned that 1 into 4/4, which you add to the 1/4 you already had, getting 5/4. Now you can subtract.

454 − 234 = 224 = 212
Pro TipIf you hate borrowing, convert both mixed numbers to improper fractions first. Then subtract. Then convert back. It works the same way, just more arithmetic.

Q2 Solve and simplify: 5 1/4 − 1 3/4
Q3 Solve: 1 1/2 + 2 1/3

Day07

Multiplying Fractions

No common denominators required. Yes, really.

Here is some good news after the past two days: multiplying fractions does NOT require finding a common denominator. You just multiply straight across. Top times top, bottom times bottom.

23 × 45 = 815

What does it actually mean?

With whole numbers, multiplication usually means "groups of." With fractions, multiplication usually means "part of." So 1/2 × 1/3 means "half of one third." And half of a third is one sixth.

This is why multiplying two proper fractions gives you a SMALLER answer. That feels weird because multiplying whole numbers usually makes things bigger. But you are taking a part of a part. That has to be smaller than where you started.

Multiplying with whole numbers

Just write the whole number as itself over 1. Then multiply normally.

4 × 23 = 41 × 23 = 83 =  223

Multiplying mixed numbers

Convert both to improper fractions first. Then multiply. Then simplify or convert back.

112 × 213 = 32 × 73 = 216 =  312
Pro TipA multiplication shortcut: if the numerator of one fraction and the denominator of the other share a factor, you can cancel BEFORE multiplying to save yourself work. Like 2/3 × 9/4: the 2 and the 4 share a factor of 2, so cancel to 1/3 × 9/2.
Q1 Solve: 1/3 × 1/5
Q2 Solve and simplify: 2/3 × 3/4
Q3 Solve: 6 × 2/3 (give your answer as a whole number)

Day08

Dividing Fractions

Keep, change, flip. The weirdest rule that actually works.

Dividing by a fraction sounds like it should be hard, but the rule is short: to divide by a fraction, multiply by its reciprocal. The reciprocal is just the fraction flipped upside down. 2/3 becomes 3/2. 5/8 becomes 8/5.

People sometimes say "Keep, Change, Flip." Keep the first fraction. Change the divide sign to a times sign. Flip the second fraction.

12 ÷ 14 = 12 × 41 =  2

Why does that work?

Think about what division asks. 1/2 ÷ 1/4 is asking "how many fourths fit inside one half?" One half is the same as two fourths, so the answer is 2. Two fourths fit. Try this with real fractions and you will see that "multiply by the reciprocal" gives the same answer every time.

Dividing whole numbers by fractions

Write the whole number over 1. Flip and multiply. So 3 ÷ 1/2 becomes 3/1 × 2/1 = 6. That makes sense: three wholes are six halves.

Dividing fractions by whole numbers

Same idea. Write the whole number over 1. Flip it and multiply. So 3/4 ÷ 2 becomes 3/4 × 1/2 = 3/8. Half of three fourths is three eighths.

34 ÷  2  = 34 × 12 = 38
Trap warningOnly flip the SECOND fraction (the one you are dividing by). Flipping the first one too is a common mistake that ruins the whole problem.

Day09

Compare and Conquer

Which fraction is bigger, and the traps fractions love to set

Sometimes you do not need to add, subtract, multiply, or divide. You just need to know which fraction is bigger. Here are four ways to figure that out without guessing.

Same denominator? Look at the top.

If the denominators match, just compare the numerators. 5/8 is bigger than 3/8 because 5 pieces of the same size beats 3 pieces of the same size.

Same numerator? Look at the bottom (carefully).

If the numerators match, the fraction with the SMALLER denominator is bigger. 1/3 is bigger than 1/6, because thirds are bigger pieces than sixths.

Different everything? Common denominator.

Rename both fractions so they have the same denominator. Then compare the numerators.

34 vs 56 → 912 vs 1012 → 56 wins

The 1/2 benchmark

One of the most useful tricks: compare each fraction to one half. If one is less than 1/2 and the other is more than 1/2, you already have your answer without doing any real math.

To check if a fraction is bigger or smaller than 1/2, double the numerator. If the doubled number is bigger than the denominator, the fraction is bigger than 1/2. If smaller, smaller.

Top five fraction traps

  1. Adding the denominators. 1/2 + 1/3 is NOT 2/5. Rename first.
  2. Thinking bigger denominator means bigger fraction. 1/10 is smaller than 1/2.
  3. Forgetting to simplify. 6/8 is not wrong, but 3/4 is the polished answer.
  4. Bad borrowing. Borrowed wholes become denominator-over-denominator, not "1/something".
  5. Flipping the wrong fraction in division. Only the divisor gets flipped.
Q1 Compare: 3/4 ___ 5/8
Q2 Compare: 5/10 ___ 1/2
Q3 Compare: 2/3 ___ 3/4

Day10

Fractions in the Wild

Word problems, real-life moves, and the big picture

Fractions are not just school math. They show up in cooking, building, sports stats, music, maps, money, medicine, science, video game design, and probability. Anywhere amounts live between whole numbers, fractions live too.

Recipe problem

A recipe uses 3/4 cup of sugar. You want to make half the recipe. How much sugar do you need? You are asking for "half OF three fourths," which is multiplication.

12 × 34 = 38 cup

Hiking problem

Maya hikes 2 and 1/3 miles in the morning and 1 and 3/4 miles in the afternoon. Total? Rename to twelfths. 2 and 4/12 + 1 and 9/12 = 3 and 13/12 = 4 and 1/12 miles total.

Woodworking problem

A board is 6 and 1/2 feet. You cut off 2 and 3/4 feet. What is left? Rename to fourths. 6 and 2/4 minus 2 and 3/4. The fraction on top is too small, so borrow. 5 and 6/4 minus 2 and 3/4 = 3 and 3/4 feet.

Ribbon problem (division)

You have 6 feet of ribbon. Each craft needs 2/3 foot. How many crafts can you make? You are asking how many 2/3 chunks fit in 6, which is division.

6  ÷ 23 = 61 × 32 =  9 crafts

The big picture

Equivalent fractions help you rename. Common denominators help you add and subtract. Simplifying tidies your answer. Improper fractions and mixed numbers are two outfits for the same number. Multiplication often means "part of." Division asks "how many of these fit inside that?"

The biggest skill is not memorizing rules. It is checking whether your answer makes sense. If you add something to 1/2 and get an answer smaller than 1/2, something is wrong. That kind of sanity check makes fractions stop being mysterious.
