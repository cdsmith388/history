DAY 01
The Builder

Multiplication is a machine that builds bigger things.

Four groups of six is twenty-four. Easy. But by the time you hit two-digit numbers, multiplication stops looking like cute little groups and starts looking like a puzzle. Today is about seeing the structure inside the puzzle.

01Start with what it really means

When you see 4 × 6, that means four groups of six. Six plus six plus six plus six. Twenty-four. Easy enough. But what about 23 × 14? You cannot just add 23 to itself fourteen times and stay sane. So instead, you break the problem apart.

This trick has a fancy name. It is called the distributive property, and it is the secret engine behind every multiplication shortcut you have ever been taught. The idea is simple: you can split numbers into friendlier pieces, multiply the pieces, and snap the answer back together.

Visual Lab // Area Model
23 × 14, broken into a rectangle

Twenty-three times fourteen is 322. You did not memorize a trick. You drew a rectangle, found the area of each piece, and added them up. That is what multiplication actually is.

02Partial products are the same idea, written down

When you write multiplication down on paper using the standard algorithm, every digit has a meaning. That little zero you put underneath when multiplying by the tens digit is not decoration. It is there because you are multiplying by twenty, not by two. When that zero disappears, the algorithm turns into a magic spell. When it stays, the spell becomes a tool.

Worked Example
346 × 27 = 346 × (20 + 7)
346 × 20 = 6,920
346 × 7 = 2,422
+ 6,920 + 2,422
= 9,342
Quick Check
Which is greater without doing the full algorithm: 25 × 16 or 50 × 8?
Reflect
Why is it useful to think of multi-digit multiplication as a rectangle rather than as a list of digit-by-digit operations?
DAY 02
The Splitter

Division asks two questions, not one.

Most people think division is just sharing. It is, but it is also something else. The same problem can mean two different things, and once you see both meanings, long division stops looking like a haunted staircase.

01The two meanings of division

Take 24 ÷ 6 = 4. That can mean: twenty-four cookies, shared among six people, gives each person four cookies. That is sharing division. But it can also mean: how many groups of six fit inside twenty-four? Four groups. That is grouping division. Same equation. Different question.

Sharing helps when you are splitting something fairly. Grouping helps when you are measuring how many chunks fit. Long division usually makes more sense if you think of it as grouping. How many groups of the divisor fit into each chunk of the dividend?

02Division is multiplication walking backward

If you know 7 × 8 = 56, then you already know 56 ÷ 8 = 7 and 56 ÷ 7 = 8. Division is not a new creature. It is multiplication wearing different clothes. That is why those multiplication facts you drilled in elementary school are not just busywork. They are how you see division faster.

Visual Lab // Two Views of 24 ÷ 6

03Long division: the loop that solves it

Long division looks like a staircase because that is exactly what it is. You walk down it one step at a time. Divide. Multiply. Subtract. Bring down. Repeat. Each step is just asking the same question over and over: how many groups fit here, and how much is left over?

984 ÷ 6, step by step
How many 6s fit in 9? One. Remainder 3.
Bring down 8. How many 6s in 38? Six. Remainder 2.
Bring down 4. How many 6s in 24? Four. Remainder 0.
= 164

When the divisor does not fit perfectly, you get a remainder. That leftover number is not garbage. It is information. If you are dividing 725 pieces of candy into 4 bags, the remainder of 1 is one candy left over. If you are dividing 725 dollars among 4 people, that remainder becomes 25 cents. If you are dividing people into cars, you cannot chop a person in half to make the math prettier. The situation decides what the remainder means.

Quick Check
A bus holds 30 students. There are 217 students going on a trip. How many buses are needed?
Reflect
Why does the same remainder behave differently in different real-world problems? Give an example where rounding up matters and one where it does not.
DAY 03
The Decimal Trick

Decimals are fractions wearing a disguise.

There is no such thing as a separate species called decimals. They are fractions written in base ten. Once that clicks, the rules stop feeling random and start feeling inevitable.

01Every decimal is a place value map

In the number 3.456, every digit has a job. The 3 is three ones. The 4 is four tenths, or 4/10. The 5 is five hundredths, or 5/100. The 6 is six thousandths, or 6/1000. Add them up and you get 3.456. The decimal point is not a separator between two different kinds of numbers. It just marks where the whole numbers end and the fractions begin.

Visual Lab // Place Value
The number 3.456 in pieces

02The classic decimal trap

Here is the question that fools a lot of people. Which is greater, 0.7 or 0.62? Some students see 62 and think it must be bigger than 7. But decimals do not work that way. Write 0.7 as 0.70. Now you are comparing 70 hundredths to 62 hundredths. Seventy is bigger. So 0.7 is bigger. The trailing zero did not change anything. It just made the comparison easier to see.

Trailing zeros are free. Adding them after the last digit of a decimal never changes the value. It just gives you matching place values to compare.

Quick Check
Which number is greatest?
Reflect
Why does adding a zero to the right of a decimal not change its value, while adding a zero to the left of a whole number changes everything?
DAY 04
Decimal Operations

Add, subtract, multiply, divide. Same rules, smarter eyes.

Once you see decimals as fractions in disguise, every operation has a reason. Lining up the dots is not a ritual. Counting decimal places is not a trick. It is all just place value, doing what place value always does.

01Adding and subtracting: line up the dots

You would not add five dollars to fifty cents by writing 5 + 50 and calling it 55. The 5 and the 50 are in different place values. The same is true for decimals. When you add 12.4 and 3.86, you line up the decimal points so that tenths add to tenths and hundredths add to hundredths.

12.4 + 3.86
  12.40
+  3.86
= 16.26

Writing 12.4 as 12.40 does not change the value. It just gives the hundredths place a visible zero so the columns line up. Same trick as before.

02Multiplying decimals: count the places

To multiply 2.4 by 1.3, first ignore the decimals and multiply like whole numbers. 24 × 13 = 312. Now stop and ask: what size should the answer actually be? 2.4 is a little more than 2. 1.3 is a little more than 1. So the answer should be a little more than 2. It cannot be 312. It cannot be 31.2. It has to be around 3 point something.

Count the decimal places in the original numbers. 2.4 has one. 1.3 has one. That makes two total. Put two decimal places in your answer. 3.12. Done. The rule is not magic. It comes from the fact that 2.4 is really 24/10 and 1.3 is really 13/10. Multiply those and you get 312/100, which is 3.12.

03Dividing decimals: the size surprise

Here is a thing nobody tells you up front: division does not always make numbers smaller. 12 ÷ 0.5 is 24. The answer is bigger than the number you started with. Why? Because you are asking how many halves fit into twelve, and there are two halves in every whole, so the answer is twice as many.

To divide by a decimal, just multiply both numbers by the same power of ten to move the decimals away. 6.4 ÷ 0.8 becomes 64 ÷ 8 = 8. The value did not change. The numbers just got easier to work with.

Quick Check
Without calculating, will 4.8 × 2.1 be closest to 1, 10, or 100?
Reflect
Most operations follow this rule: bigger numbers in, bigger numbers out. Why does dividing by a number smaller than 1 break that rule?
DAY 05
Fractions, Part One

Fractions are not pizza slices. They are numbers.

The pizza example works fine at first. But if every fraction is a pizza, math gets weird fast. A fraction can be a part of a whole, a division problem, a point on a number line, a ratio, or a scale factor. Same notation, many jobs.

01Three things that fraction means at once

The fraction 34 means three parts when something is split into four equal pieces. It also means 3 ÷ 4. And it also names a specific number that sits between 0 and 1 on the number line. All three are true at the same time.

The number on top is called the numerator. The number on the bottom is the denominator. The denominator tells you how big the pieces are. The numerator tells you how many of those pieces you have.

Visual Lab // Same Value, Different Outfits
These are all the same number

02Equivalent fractions are the same number, dressed differently

The fractions 12, 24, 36, and 48 are all equivalent fractions. They look different. They name the same number. You make equivalent fractions by multiplying the top and bottom by the same number. Since that is just multiplying by 1 in disguise, the value never changes.

03Simplifying is cleaning up the handwriting

When you simplify 1218, you find a number that divides both the top and the bottom. Six works. 12 ÷ 6 = 2 and 18 ÷ 6 = 3. So 1218 simplifies to 23. The fraction did not shrink. The writing got cleaner.

Quick Check
Which fraction is equivalent to 6/8?
Reflect
If 1/2 and 50/100 are the same number, why bother with the simpler form? Are there ever times the bigger form is more useful?
DAY 06
Combining Fractions

To add fractions, the pieces have to match.

You cannot add three apples and two oranges and call it five apples. You also cannot add thirds and fourths until you turn them into the same-sized pieces. That is the whole secret to adding fractions.

01Same denominator? Just add the tops

When the bottoms already match, you are counting same-sized pieces. 38 plus 28 is just three eighths plus two eighths, which is five eighths. The denominator stays the same because the size of the pieces did not change. You are not adding 8 plus 8. You are adding 3 plus 2 in eighth-sized units.

02Different denominators? Find a common one

To add 23 plus 14, you cannot just add 2 + 1 and 3 + 4 and call it 37. Thirds and fourths are not the same size. You need a common denominator. The smallest number that both 3 and 4 divide into is 12. So rewrite both fractions over 12.

2/3 + 1/4
2/3 = 8/12 (multiply top and bottom by 4)
1/4 = 3/12 (multiply top and bottom by 3)
8/12 + 3/12 = 11/12
= 11/12

03Mixed numbers and improper fractions are the same animal

A mixed number like 3 1/2 just means three wholes plus half of another. You can also write it as 72, which is called an improper fraction. Three wholes is six halves. Six halves plus one half is seven halves. The name "improper" sounds suspicious, but improper fractions are totally legal math. They are often easier to multiply and divide.

Quick Check
What is 1/2 + 1/3?
Reflect
Why does the denominator stay the same when you add fractions with matching denominators, but everything has to be rewritten when they do not match?
DAY 07
Part of a Part

Multiplying fractions means taking part of a part.

When you multiply whole numbers, the answer gets bigger. When you multiply by a fraction less than one, the answer often gets smaller. That feels backwards until you see what is actually happening.

01The shortcut and why it works

To multiply two fractions, multiply the tops together and multiply the bottoms together. So 23 times 45 becomes 815. That is the rule. But the rule by itself is forgettable. The reason behind it is not.

Try 12 times 13. In words: half of one third. Draw a rectangle. Split it into thirds going one way. Then split it into halves going the other way. You now have six little equal pieces. The one piece that is both halved and thirded is one out of six. So 1/2 × 1/3 = 1/6. The math matches the picture exactly.

Visual Lab // 2/3 × 4/5
The overlap is the answer

02Why does the answer get smaller?

When you multiply by a number less than 1, you are taking a piece of something. You are not making it bigger. You are making it shorter. 10 × 1/2 is just half of 10, which is 5. The "times" sign here is doing exactly what the word "of" does. Ten times one half is the same as one half of ten.

Quick Check
What is 3/4 × 2/5?
Reflect
Multiplication usually makes numbers bigger. Why is it that multiplying by a fraction less than one makes them smaller? Where else in math does this kind of surprise happen?
DAY 08
How Many Fit?

Dividing fractions asks: how many of these fit inside that?

You probably learned the rule. Keep, change, flip. It works. But if you only know the rule and not the question behind it, the rule looks like a magic spell. Let us look behind the curtain.

01The question is always the same

When you write 3/4 ÷ 1/2, you are not asking some mysterious thing. You are asking: how many halves fit into three fourths? Draw it. One half fits. Then half of another half fits. So the answer is one and one half. The "keep, change, flip" trick gives the same answer: 3/4 × 2/1 = 6/4 = 1 1/2. The trick works because flipping a fraction and multiplying is the same as dividing.

Visual Lab // 5 ÷ 1/2
How many halves fit into 5 whole things?

02Keep, change, flip, in plain English

"Keep" means leave the first fraction alone. "Change" means turn division into multiplication. "Flip" means use the reciprocal of the second fraction. The reciprocal of 2/3 is 3/2. Flip them upside down. Then multiply like normal.

4/7 ÷ 2/3
Keep: 4/7
Change ÷ to ×
Flip: 2/3 becomes 3/2
4/7 × 3/2 = 12/14 = 6/7
= 6/7

03Why dividing by a small fraction makes a big answer

If you divide 10 by 2, you get 5. If you divide 10 by 1, you get 10. If you divide 10 by 1/2, you get 20. As the divisor gets smaller, the answer gets bigger. That is because you are asking how many of the divisor fit, and smaller pieces fit more times. It feels weird, but it is the same rule applied consistently.

Quick Check
What is 1/2 ÷ 1/4?
Reflect
If "keep, change, flip" is just a trick that gives the right answer, why teach the question first? What does the question tell you that the rule does not?
DAY 09
Rules of the Road

Ratios compare. Order of operations keeps order.

Today is about structure. How to compare two things using ratios, and how to read an expression with multiple operations without getting tangled up in your own arithmetic.

01Ratios compare quantities

A ratio compares two amounts. If a recipe uses 2 cups of flour and 3 cups of oats, the ratio of flour to oats is 2:3, which can also be written 2 to 3 or 23. That means for every 2 cups of flour, there are 3 cups of oats. The ratio holds even if you scale the whole recipe up. Double everything and the ratio is now 4:6, which is still 2:3.

02Unit rates: how much per one?

A unit rate is a ratio with the second part equal to 1. If a car travels 180 miles in 3 hours, divide 180 by 3 to get 60. That is the unit rate: 60 miles per hour. The car travels 60 miles in 1 hour. Unit rates are how you compare different deals at the grocery store, different cars on the highway, and different running paces on a track.

Visual Lab // Ratio Table
60 miles per hour

03Order of operations: math needs traffic rules

If everyone solved expressions in their own order, the same problem would have different answers depending on who was holding the pencil. That would be chaos. So math has rules. PEMDAS is the standard acronym. Parentheses first. Then exponents. Then multiplication and division, working left to right. Then addition and subtraction, also left to right.

The left-to-right part matters. Multiplication does not always happen before division. They are partners. Same with addition and subtraction. They go in the order they appear.

18 ÷ 3 × 2 + 5
Division and multiplication first, left to right:
18 ÷ 3 = 6
6 × 2 = 12
Then addition:
12 + 5
= 17

Parentheses change everything. 4 + 3 × 2 equals 10 because the multiplication happens first. But (4 + 3) × 2 equals 14 because the parentheses force the addition to go first. Same numbers, different structure, different answer. Parentheses are not decoration. They are commands.

Quick Check
What is 6 + 4 × 3?
Reflect
Why is left-to-right ordering for multiplication and division so important? Try working out 12 ÷ 4 × 3 both ways and see what you get.
DAY 10
The Mystery Number

Algebra is just arithmetic with a question mark in it.

Algebra is not some new alien math that begins later. It is the math you already know, written with one number missing. Your job is to find the missing number. Everything else stays the same.

01An equation is a balance scale

When you see x + 7 = 19, picture a balance scale. On one side: some unknown number plus 7. On the other side: 19. The two sides are equal. Whatever you do to one side, you have to do to the other, or the scale tips. To find the mystery number, undo the +7 by subtracting 7 from both sides. Now you have x = 12. Mystery solved.

Visual Lab // The Balance
Whatever you do to one side, do to the other

02Use the opposite operation

Every operation has an opposite. Addition and subtraction are opposites. Multiplication and division are opposites. To find the mystery number, do the opposite of whatever is happening to it.

Solve 5x = 40
x is being multiplied by 5. Do the opposite: divide.
5x ÷ 5 = 40 ÷ 5
x = 8
Solve x ÷ 3 = 9
x is being divided by 3. Do the opposite: multiply.
x ÷ 3 × 3 = 9 × 3
x = 27

03Two-step equations work the same way

For 3x + 4 = 19, peel the operations off in reverse order. The mystery number got multiplied by 3, and then 4 got added. Reverse that. First, subtract 4 from both sides to get 3x = 15. Then divide both sides by 3 to get x = 5. It is just the balance scale, two steps at a time.

Quick Check
Solve x + 9 = 22
Reflect
Look back at everything from Day 1 to Day 10. Where do you see multiplication, division, fractions, decimals, ratios, and equations all touching each other? Math is not separate islands. Where are the bridges?
