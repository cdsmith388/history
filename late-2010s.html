RUSSIA, MEMES & THE NEW POLITICAL MACHINE

How the internet became a battlefield 💀

The late 2010s started with a wild realization: the internet wasn't just for cat videos and funny tweets anymore. It had become a full-on political battlefield. After the 2016 U.S. election, one of the biggest issues in America was Russian interference.

Special Counsel Robert Mueller's 2019 report concluded that Russia interfered in the 2016 election in a "sweeping and systematic fashion." Russia hacked Democratic emails AND ran disinformation campaigns on social media to divide Americans.

Special Counsel Robert Mueller testifying before Congress, 2019

💡 This didn't mean every online argument was from Russia — America already had big divisions. But it showed how foreign countries could exploit social media to mess with us.

Then there were memes. At first, internet memes were just silly: cats, reaction faces, weird jokes. But by the late 2010s, memes became political weapons too. They could simplify complex issues, mock opponents, build group identity, or make extreme ideas seem like jokes.

The scary part? A false claim could go viral faster than the truth. A foreign influence operation and a teenager in their bedroom were competing in the same feed — and sometimes it was hard to tell them apart.

The full decade of internet memes — from Troll Face to OK Boomer

CRYPTO, NET NEUTRALITY, SMARTPHONES & TikTok

When the internet got in your pocket and your wallet 📱

Cryptocurrency moved from a nerdy internet idea into the mainstream. Bitcoin had been invented in 2008 by the mysterious Satoshi Nakamoto, but in the late 2010s it exploded into headlines. Supporters said crypto could challenge banks and give financial freedom to anyone. Critics pointed to scams, wild price swings, and massive energy use.

Americans also fought over net neutrality — the idea that internet providers should treat all web traffic equally. In 2017, the FCC under Chairman Ajit Pai voted to repeal the 2015 net neutrality rules. Supporters of net neutrality worried that without it, companies like Comcast or Verizon could speed up their own video services and slow down competitors like Netflix or YouTube.

An algorithm: rules that turn input into output. TikTok's algorithm turns your watch history into your next video.

📱 By the late 2010s, the smartphone wasn't just a phone. It was your camera, map, wallet, alarm clock, classroom distraction, music player, news feed, social stage, and argument machine — all in one device.

And then came TikTok. After ByteDance merged with Musical.ly in 2017, TikTok's rise was unstoppable. But unlike older platforms where you mostly saw what friends posted, TikTok's algorithm learned exactly what kept each person glued to the screen and served up an endless feed of it.

Concerns exploded: too much screen time, body image problems for teens, misinformation, and — because TikTok's parent company ByteDance is based in China — questions about data privacy and foreign influence.

TikTok's feed — endless short videos powered by a recommendation algorithm

THE BIRTH OF MODERN AI & FIRST PHOTO OF A BLACK HOLE

Machines learning to see. Scientists seeing the unseeable. 🌌

Artificial intelligence didn't start in the 2010s — researchers had been working on it since the 1950s. But the late 2010s felt like the beginning of the modern AI era because deep learning systems became shockingly powerful.

A huge turning point came in 2012 when AlexNet — built by Alex Krizhevsky, Ilya Sutskever, and Geoffrey Hinton — blew away the competition in a massive image-recognition challenge. It was trained on 1.2 million photos and could identify objects far better than any AI before it.

By the late 2010s, AI was everywhere: translation apps, facial recognition, recommendation systems, ads that knew your interests, voice assistants, search engines, and medical imaging. But AI wasn't neutral. It could inherit bias from training data and make invisible mistakes at scale.

AlexNet was a turning point in the history of AI — the leap that made modern deep learning possible

🔭 So the late 2010s had two kinds of machine vision: one that looked AT us through phones and algorithms — and one that looked across 55 million light-years of space.

In April 2019, scientists did something that seemed impossible: they released the first photograph of a black hole. The Event Horizon Telescope — a network of radio telescopes linked across the entire planet — captured the black hole at the center of galaxy Messier 87, some 55 million light-years away. The image showed a glowing ring around pure darkness. It was real.

The first-ever photograph of a black hole — galaxy M87, April 10, 2019. 55 million light-years away.

U.S. vs. CHINA: THE RETURN OF GREAT-POWER COMPETITION

Trade wars, tech battles, and two superpowers watching each other 👀

For years, many U.S. leaders believed that trading with China would make China more open and democratic over time. By the late 2010s, that hope had faded. China had become a manufacturing superpower, a technological force, and a growing military power. The U.S. worried about trade deficits, intellectual property theft, cyberespionage, and China's rapid military expansion in the South China Sea.

In 2017, the U.S. National Security Strategy called China and Russia revisionist powers that challenged American power. This was a major shift — from seeing China mostly as a trading partner to seeing China as a strategic rival.

The Trump administration responded by putting tariffs on Chinese goods. China fired back. This back-and-forth is called a trade war. But the competition went way beyond economics. It was also about who controls 5G networks, semiconductors, AI, data, and the platforms billions of people use every day.

The U.S.–China rivalry became one of the defining geopolitical stories of the late 2010s

🤔 The old Cold War was U.S. vs. Soviet Union. But this rivalry was different — the U.S. and China were deeply economically connected. They competed AND depended on each other at the same time.

Questions that once sounded like sci-fi were now national security issues: Who controls the data? Who builds the chips? Who writes the rules for the internet? Who can shut off access in a crisis? The TikTok debate was really part of this bigger rivalry.

COVID-19 APPEARS AT THE EDGE OF THE DECADE

December 31, 2019. A disease notice that changed everything. 🌍

At the very end of 2019, the decade gave one final warning. On December 31, 2019, Chinese officials reported a cluster of mysterious pneumonia cases in Wuhan, Hubei Province to the World Health Organization. The WHO reported that by January 3, Chinese authorities had told them about 44 patients with pneumonia of unknown cause — 11 severely ill, 33 stable.

At first it sounded like a distant local health alert. But it was the beginning of COVID-19, the disease caused by SARS-CoV-2. Within months it became a global pandemic.

⚠️ The timing mattered enormously. COVID arrived into a world already built by the late 2010s: misinformation traveled at internet speed, governments were already distrusted, U.S.–China tensions were rising, and everyone had a smartphone — meaning every development would be filmed, shared, debated, and distorted in real time.

The pandemic that followed wasn't only a medical crisis. It was an information crisis (what's true?), a trust crisis (who do I believe?), an economic crisis (shutdowns, job losses), and a political crisis. Every problem of the late 2010s — polarization, misinformation, social media speed, U.S.–China tension — would shape how the world experienced COVID-19.
