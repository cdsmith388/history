01 / FORCES UNSEEN

What is Magnetism?

A magnet can pull a paperclip across a table without touching it. The pull is invisible. The pull works through air, through paper, sometimes through your hand. Whatever the pull is, it is real, and it has a name.

Magnetism is a force that can pull some objects closer or push some objects away without touching them. It is one part of a bigger force called the electromagnetic force, which is one of the four basic forces in nature.

Magnetism is closely connected to moving electric charges. That means electricity and magnetism are not separate subjects at all. They are deeply linked. The same force that holds a magnet to your refrigerator also helps run electric motors, spin generators, paint auroras in the sky, and steer the needle of a compass.

Everyday magnetism. Each small magnet creates an invisible field strong enough to hold paper to steel.

A force that does not need contact to act is called a non-contact force. Gravity is also a non-contact force. So is static electricity. Magnetism belongs to this special family.

STOP AND THINK

The reach of a magnet

If a magnet does not touch a paperclip, what is doing the pulling? Where does the pull come from?

The magnet creates an invisible region around itself called a magnetic field. The field reaches out through the space around the magnet. When the paperclip enters that field, the field pulls on tiny magnetic regions inside the clip itself. The field, not the metal of the magnet, is doing the work.
02 / NORTH AND SOUTH

The Two Poles

Every magnet, no matter how big or small, has two ends. These ends are not the same. They behave differently from each other, and we have to give them names.

The two ends of a magnet are called its poles. One is the north pole. The other is the south pole. These names come from the way magnets line up with the Earth, which we will get to soon.

Every magnet has two poles. The invisible field curves out from one pole and back into the other.
Opposite poles attract. Like poles repel.

That single rule explains how magnets behave around each other. Hold the north end of one magnet near the south end of another, and they snap together. Flip one magnet over, and they fight each other. They will not come close. The push between them feels almost springy, as if the air between them has turned solid.

Opposites attract. Likes push apart. This rule is true no matter the size or shape of the magnet.

The strange truth about cutting a magnet

Here is a fact that surprises most people. If you cut a bar magnet in half, you do not get a north piece and a south piece. You get two smaller magnets, each with its own north and south pole. Cut those in half, and you get four magnets. Keep cutting, and every piece is still a magnet with two poles.

Scientists have searched for a single-pole magnet, called a magnetic monopole, for a long time. They have not found one in any ordinary material.

STOP AND THINK

Predict the push

Two bar magnets sit on a table. Both have their north ends facing each other. You let go. What happens? Now flip one magnet around so its south end faces the first magnet's north. What happens this time?

In the first case, the magnets push apart because like poles repel. In the second case, opposite poles attract, so they snap together. The amazing thing is that the force is the same strength either way. It just changes direction depending on which poles are facing.
03 / THE INVISIBLE SHAPE

Mapping the Magnetic Field

You cannot see a magnetic field. But you can make it visible.

Place a sheet of paper over a bar magnet. Sprinkle iron filings on top. Tap the paper gently. The tiny filings will line up in curved patterns that flow from one end of the magnet to the other. They are showing you something that was there all along.

Iron filings reveal the magnetic field. Each filing acts as a tiny compass and points along the field.

Scientists describe the field using magnetic field lines. These are not real strings or wires. They are a drawing tool. But they tell you three important things at once.

  • Field lines curve from one pole of a magnet to the other.
  • Where the lines are crowded together, the field is strong.
  • Where the lines spread apart, the field is weaker.
  • The lines show the direction a compass would point if you placed it there.

Because a magnetic field has both a strength and a direction, scientists call it a vector quantity. That sounds technical, but it just means that telling someone about a field requires two pieces of information, not one.

STOP AND THINK

A field with no eyes

Iron filings clearly show field lines. But the field was there before you sprinkled the filings. Is the field still real if no filings are on the paper?

Yes. The field exists whether or not anything is there to detect it. The filings only make it visible to us. This is one of the strangest and most important ideas in science: there are real things in the world that we cannot see directly, but we can prove they are there by what they do.
04 / WHAT STICKS

Why Some Things Stick (and Most Don't)

A magnet does not pick up everything. Try it. A magnet ignores wood, plastic, paper, glass, cotton, and even a lot of metals.

Aluminum foil? Nothing. A copper penny? Nothing. Gold and silver? Nothing. But touch a magnet to a steel nail, a paperclip, or an iron washer, and suddenly the magnet has a grip.

A magnet picks up steel paperclips eagerly. The colorful coating does not matter. The steel underneath does.

The materials a magnet sticks to strongly are mostly iron, nickel, cobalt, and many kinds of steel. These materials are called ferromagnetic. The word comes from ferrum, which is the Latin word for iron.

Material Type What It Does
Ferromagnetic Strongly attracted to magnets. Can often become a magnet itself. Examples: iron, nickel, cobalt, steel.
Paramagnetic Weakly attracted to magnetic fields. The effect is too small to feel in most cases.
Diamagnetic Weakly repelled by magnetic fields. Even more subtle than paramagnetism.
Nonmagnetic Not noticeably affected by an ordinary magnet. Examples: paper, plastic, wood, glass.

So why do iron and nickel feel a pull when copper and gold do not? The answer hides inside the atoms themselves.

05 / ZOOM IN

Magnetic Domains

Imagine zooming into a piece of iron until you can see groups of atoms. You find tiny regions where many atoms are lined up the same way. These regions are the secret behind every magnet.

Electrons inside atoms behave a bit like tiny magnets themselves. In most materials, these tiny atomic magnets point in random directions and cancel each other out. The material has no overall magnetism.

But in ferromagnetic materials like iron, groups of atoms can lock together and point in the same direction. These groups are called magnetic domains.

Unmagnetized iron has domains pointing in all directions. In a magnet, the domains have lined up.

When the domains point every which way, they cancel each other out, and the piece of iron is not magnetic. But if you stroke a steel needle with a magnet, you push many of the domains into the same direction. Now the needle has more domains lined up than not. The needle has become a magnet.

This is also why dropping a magnet on the floor or heating it up can weaken it. The shock or heat shakes the domains and lets some of them slip back out of alignment.

STOP AND THINK

A reversible secret

If a strong magnet can magnetize a needle by lining up its domains, what do you think would happen if you heated that needle in a flame and then let it cool with no magnet nearby?

The heat shakes the atoms so violently that the domains scramble back to random directions. When the needle cools, the domains stay scrambled. The needle is no longer a magnet. This is one way to demagnetize something on purpose.
06 / FOREVER AND FLEETING

Permanent and Temporary

Not all magnets last forever. Some only act like magnets for a moment.

Permanent Magnets

A permanent magnet keeps its magnetism for years. The domains stay locked in place. Examples: refrigerator magnets, bar magnets, speaker magnets, the magnet inside a compass.

Temporary Magnets

A temporary magnet acts magnetic only while something is making it magnetic. A paperclip touching a strong magnet briefly becomes one. Pull the magnet away, and the paperclip mostly loses its magnetism.

There is a third kind of magnet that is the most useful of all. It does not exist naturally. We had to invent it.

07 / THE GREAT DISCOVERY

Electricity Meets Magnetism

For most of history, people thought electricity and magnetism were two separate mysteries. Lightning was one strange force. Lodestones were another. No one suspected they were two sides of the same coin.

In April of 1820, a Danish scientist named Hans Christian Ørsted was setting up a classroom demonstration. He happened to switch on an electric current in a wire near a compass. The compass needle twitched. Ørsted stopped. He tried it again. The needle moved again.

Electricity could move a compass. That meant electricity creates magnetism. The two forces were one.

Building a magnet from a battery

Once people realized electricity could make magnetism, they figured out how to build a magnet you could switch on and off. It is called an electromagnet.

The recipe is simple. Wrap an insulated wire around an iron nail many times. Connect the wire to a battery. Current flows through the coil, and the nail becomes a magnet strong enough to pick up paperclips. Disconnect the battery, and the nail goes back to being just a nail.

A battery, a coil of wire, and an iron nail. Together they make a magnet you can switch on and off.
Feature Permanent Magnet Electromagnet
Needs electricity? No Yes
Can be turned off? Not easily Yes, instantly
Strength can be changed? Not really Yes, easily
Common use Fridge magnet Junkyard crane, motors, doorbells

There are three main ways to make an electromagnet stronger:

  • Wrap more coils of wire around the core.
  • Send more current through the wire.
  • Put a ferromagnetic core, such as an iron nail, inside the coil.
When current loops through a coil, the magnetic field of each loop adds together. This shape is called a solenoid.
STOP AND THINK

The junkyard crane

A junkyard crane uses a giant electromagnet to lift cars onto a pile. Why would they use an electromagnet instead of a permanent magnet for this job?

They need to drop the car when they reach the pile. With a permanent magnet, you would have to pry the car off somehow. With an electromagnet, the operator just flips a switch. The current stops, the magnetism stops, and the car falls free. Being able to turn the magnet off is the whole point.
08 / MOTION AND ELECTRICITY

Motors and Generators

Once you know that electricity creates magnetism, you can flip the idea around. Can magnetism create electricity? Yes. And that single trick powers nearly the entire modern world.

When a magnet moves near a coil of wire, or when a coil of wire moves near a magnet, the electrons in the wire start moving. Moving electrons are an electric current. This process is called electromagnetic induction.

In a generator, magnets and coils work together. As something spins, electric current flows out.

A generator uses this idea on purpose. Something spins the coil. The coil moves through a magnetic field. Electricity flows out of the coil into wires that carry the current to homes, schools, and hospitals.

A generator does not create energy out of thin air. It converts motion energy into electrical energy. Where the motion comes from depends on the power plant.

In a hydroelectric dam, falling water spins a turbine. The turbine spins a coil inside a generator. Magnetism turns motion into electricity.

A motor runs the trick backward

A motor does the opposite job of a generator. A motor takes in electricity and produces motion. The same coils, the same magnets, but the energy flows the other way.

Generator

Motion in → Electricity out. Used in power plants, wind turbines, hydroelectric dams, and the alternator in a car engine.

Motor

Electricity in → Motion out. Used in fans, washing machines, electric cars, blenders, hard drives, and power tools.

Almost every machine that spins, lifts, or pushes runs on a motor. Almost every spark of electricity in your house came from a generator. Magnetism stands quietly behind all of it.

09 / THE PLANET AS A MAGNET

Earth: A Giant Magnet

Hold a compass anywhere on Earth. The needle swings around and points roughly toward the north. Why? Because the entire planet is acting like one enormous magnet.

Earth has its own magnetic field, with a north magnetic pole and a south magnetic pole. The needle of a compass is itself a small magnet. It lines up with Earth's field, the same way iron filings line up around a bar magnet.

Earth's magnetic field. The magnetic poles are tilted away from the geographic poles, which is why compasses do not point to true north.
A compass needle is a tiny floating magnet. It rotates until it lines up with Earth's magnetic field.

True north is not magnetic north

Here is something hikers and pilots have to remember. Earth's magnetic pole is not in the same place as the geographic North Pole. They are close, but not the same point. The geographic North Pole is the top of the planet's spin axis. The magnetic north pole is wherever Earth's magnetic field is pointing straight up out of the ground.

The angle between true north and magnetic north is called magnetic declination. It is different in different places, and it slowly changes over time, because the magnetic poles drift.

Where does Earth's magnetism come from?

Deep inside Earth, beneath the crust and the mantle, is a hot ball of mostly iron and nickel. The outer part of this core is liquid. As Earth rotates, that hot liquid metal moves and swirls. Moving electrically conducting fluid creates electric currents, and those currents create Earth's magnetic field. This process is called the geodynamo.

Earth is not magnetic the way a refrigerator magnet is. Its magnetism is generated, every second, by the constant churning of metal far below your feet.

10 / THE INVISIBLE SHIELD

The Magnetosphere

Earth's magnetic field does not stop at the surface. It stretches out into space, forming a bubble around the planet. This bubble is called the magnetosphere, and it is one of the most important things keeping you alive.

The Sun is constantly throwing charged particles into space at incredible speeds. This stream is called the solar wind. The solar wind hits Earth's magnetic field and gets deflected around it, like a stream of water flowing around a rock.

The solar wind squashes Earth's field on the side facing the Sun and stretches it out on the night side into the long magnetotail.

On the side facing the Sun, the magnetosphere is squished close to the planet. On the night side, it gets stretched out into a long tail, called the magnetotail, which trails behind Earth for an enormous distance.

Without this shield, the solar wind would slowly strip away Earth's atmosphere over millions of years, the way it has done to Mars. Magnetism is part of why our planet still has air to breathe.

11 / LIGHT IN THE SKY

Auroras and Space Weather

Sometimes the sky near the poles glows green and pink and purple. This is the aurora. It is one of the most beautiful sights on Earth, and it is made by magnetism.

An aurora is sunlight turned into a glow by Earth's magnetic field.

Although the magnetosphere blocks most of the solar wind, some charged particles slip through near the poles and follow Earth's magnetic field lines down into the upper atmosphere. When they crash into atoms of oxygen and nitrogen, those atoms get energized and then release the extra energy as light.

Different gases glow in different colors. Oxygen makes green and red. Nitrogen makes blue and purple. The whole show happens about 100 kilometers above the ground.

Auroras usually only appear near the poles, which is why you hear them called the northern lights or southern lights. During strong solar storms, though, auroras can sometimes be seen much farther from the poles, sometimes even in the southern United States.

Magnetic storms

When the Sun has a big outburst, the extra solar wind can shake Earth's magnetic field hard. This is called a geomagnetic storm. Beyond making bright auroras, a strong storm can cause:

  • Static and interference on radio signals.
  • GPS errors that throw off navigation.
  • Extra electric currents in power grids that can damage equipment.
  • Trouble for satellites in orbit.

Space weather forecasters watch the Sun the way regular weather forecasters watch clouds.

12 / FROZEN MEMORY

Rocks That Remember

Earth's magnetic field has not always pointed the same way. Sometimes it flips. North becomes south. South becomes north. And rocks remember every flip.

When lava erupts from a volcano and cools into rock, magnetic minerals inside it line up with Earth's magnetic field at that moment. Once the rock hardens, the minerals stay locked in place. The rock has frozen a snapshot of the field.

Scientists who study this magnetic record are doing a kind of detective work called paleomagnetism.

As new ocean floor forms at a mid-ocean ridge, alternating stripes of normal and reversed magnetism stretch out on both sides like pages in a book.

The strangest discovery from these magnetic records is that Earth's magnetic poles have flipped many times over the planet's history. This is called a polarity reversal. The last flip happened about 780,000 years ago.

The matching pattern of magnetic stripes on either side of mid-ocean ridges was one of the strongest pieces of evidence for the theory of plate tectonics. Magnetism helped prove that the continents move.

13 / THE STORY OF MAGNETISM

A Brief History

People have known about magnetic stones for thousands of years. But understanding what magnetism really is took much, much longer.

Era Discovery
Ancient world Naturally magnetic stones called lodestones were noticed in places like Magnesia in Greece. The word "magnet" comes from that name.
Medieval period Sailors in China and Europe began using magnetized needles as compasses. This made long-distance ocean travel possible.
1600 English scientist William Gilbert published the idea that Earth itself acts like a giant magnet.
1820 Hans Christian Ørsted discovered that electric current moves a compass needle. Electricity and magnetism were finally connected.
1830s Michael Faraday discovered electromagnetic induction. He showed magnetism could make electricity.
1860s James Clerk Maxwell wrote down the equations that describe electricity, magnetism, and light all together.
Today Magnetism powers motors, generators, hard drives, MRI machines, maglev trains, and almost every piece of electronics around you.
14 / GETTING IT RIGHT

Things People Get Wrong

Magnetism is full of surprises. Here are some common ideas that sound right but are not.

"All metals are magnetic."

False. Aluminum, copper, gold, and silver are all metals, and none of them stick to an ordinary magnet. Only iron, nickel, cobalt, and many steels are strongly magnetic.

"Bigger magnets are always stronger."

False. Strength depends on the material, the shape, the temperature, and how well the domains line up. A small rare-earth magnet can be far stronger than a huge ceramic one.

"Field lines are real strings."

False. Field lines are a drawing tool. The field itself is real, but the lines we draw are just a way to show its shape and direction on paper.

"Magnetic north is the North Pole."

False. Magnetic north and true north are in different places. The difference is called magnetic declination, and it changes over time.

15 / PUTTING IT TOGETHER

Magnetism, Everywhere

Look around the room you are in. Magnetism is doing work right now, all around you.

The speakers in your phone use tiny electromagnets to push and pull a paper cone, making the air vibrate so you hear sound. The hard drive in a computer uses microscopic magnetic regions to store every photo, video, and document. The motor in a fan spins because of magnetic forces between coils and magnets.

Outside your house, transformers on power poles use coils and magnetic cores to send electricity safely down wires. Cars contain dozens of small motors and sensors that depend on magnetism. Doctors use giant magnetic machines called MRIs to take pictures inside your body without cutting it open.

And far overhead, Earth's magnetic field is still doing its quiet work. It shields the planet from solar wind. It lights up the auroras. It steers some birds, sea turtles, and salmon on their journeys. It points the needle of every compass.

A force you cannot see. A pull through empty space. A simple rule about opposites and likes. From that one idea grew nearly everything modern.

You have just walked through one of the most powerful and least obvious forces in the universe. Now let's see what you remember.
