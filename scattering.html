Chapter 1 Sun overhead

Sunlight is more colorful than it looks

Take the air away and the daytime sky goes black. That single fact tells you where the blue is coming from.

Chapter 1 · Step 1

Turn off the air for a second

Imagine you could pull Earth's away at noon. The Sun would still be blinding. But look anywhere else and you would see black, with stars, in the middle of the day.

Astronauts see exactly this. So the blue you know is not coming from the Sun and it is not coming from space.

The sky does not make its own blue light. Air redirects sunlight toward your eyes.

Chapter 1 · Step 3

Now run the prism backwards

If a prism can pull white light apart, the pieces should go back together. Aim a red lamp, a green lamp and a blue lamp at the same white wall and let the circles overlap. Where two of them land you get a color neither lamp is emitting. Where all three land you get white. This is additive mixing, and it is the opposite of what happens when you mix paint.

Red, green and blue are called the primary colors of light for one reason only: those three are spaced out well enough to reach all three kinds of receptor in your eye. There is nothing special about them physically. Nature did not pick them. Your retina did.

Which leads to something you should be suspicious of. Three lamps making white is not the same thing as sunlight making white. Sunlight carries every wavelength in the spectrum at once. Three lamps carry three. They look identical to you because your cone cells only report three numbers, and both mixtures happen to produce the same three numbers. Hold on to that. Chapter 2 turns on exactly this fact to explain why a sky full of violet light still looks blue.

Chapter 1 · Step 4

Something that makes no light of its own

The lamps on the bench were sources. They made light. Now try the opposite case, which is far more common in the universe: an object that makes nothing and simply hands back whatever it is given.

The Moon is rock. Dark rock, closer to worn asphalt than to snow. It has no fire in it. Every photon you have ever seen from the Moon started at the Sun, crossed 150 million kilometers, bounced off dust, and came to you. So the Moon cannot choose its color. Whatever wavelengths arrive are the only wavelengths that can leave.

Switch the bands below on and off and watch what the Moon is forced to do. This is not a trick or a filter over the image. The disk color is computed from whatever spectrum you send it.

Why this matters twice over

Chapter 4 comes back to this rig with real physics behind it. A low Moon looks orange because the long path through air removed the short wavelengths before the light ever reached your eye. A Moon in total eclipse looks copper because the only light reaching it has been bent through Earth's atmosphere, which stripped the blue out on the way. In both cases the Moon did nothing. Something upstream edited the spectrum, and the Moon obediently reported the result.

Chapter 1 · Step 6

What the light has to travel through

Everything from here on happens inside a mixture. So it is worth knowing what the mixture actually is, and how lopsided it is.

Build dry air yourself in the panel below. Drag each gas into the column, biggest share first if you can, and see how quickly you run out of room. Two gases take almost the whole thing. and together are about 99 percent of dry air, which is why those two molecules do nearly all of the color sorting that follows.

The rest is a rounding error by volume and still matters enormously for other reasons. Carbon dioxide is 0.04 percent of the air and runs the planet's temperature. Ozone is a few parts per million and blocks the ultraviolet that would otherwise sterilize the surface. Small is not the same as unimportant. It is only unimportant for the specific question we are asking here, which is what scatters visible light.

Chapter 1 · Step 7

What "scattering" actually means

does not destroy light. It turns light. A light wave carries a fast flipping electric field, and when that field reaches a molecule it nudges the molecule's electrons back and forth. The molecule briefly behaves like a microscopic antenna and sends light back out in new directions.

Because the incoming wave is driving the whole thing, the light that leaves keeps almost exactly the wavelength it had. It changes direction, not identity. So the molecules are not blue. They are just better at turning short waves than long ones.

The viewer below runs three different things that all get loosely called "light coming off matter." They are not the same process, and the difference is the point. Switch between them and read what actually happens to the energy in each case.

One molecule, close up three processes, one comparison

The atom absorbs energy, holds it, then releases a brand new photon at a wavelength set by the gap between its energy levels. The outgoing color depends on the atom, not on whatever went in. Energy changes hands. Timing is delayed. Color is reset.

A droplet is not tiny compared with the wave. It is thousands of wavelengths across, so different parts of its surface send light back slightly out of step with each other. Those overlapping waves reinforce strongly straight ahead and cancel in most other directions. That produces the narrow forward beam you see, and it happens at almost the same strength for every visible color. No color preference, so the result is white. This is why clouds, fog and steam are white while the clear sky is blue.

Why the sky looks blue

Chapter 2 · Step 2

The rule, and why it hits so hard

Rayleigh scattering is roughly proportional to 1 divided by wavelength to the fourth power, written as 1/λ⁴, where the Greek letter lambda means wavelength.

The fourth power is the whole story. A modest change in wavelength becomes a huge change in scattering. Blue near 450 nm scatters roughly four times more than red near 650 nm, even though those numbers look close together.

Drag both sliders and watch the ratio. Try 400 against 700 and see what a factor of nine looks like.

The fourth power rule 1 / λ⁴

Why a fourth power is so violent

A fourth power means small differences in wavelength turn into large differences in scattering. Blue is only about 1.4 times shorter than red. Raise 1.4 to the fourth power and you get roughly 4. That single step from "a bit shorter" to "four times as scattered" is the entire reason the sky has a color instead of being a dim gray haze.

Compare the exponents yourself. The physics says four. Nothing else gives you the sky you actually live under.

Sky would look
Where the fourth power comes from

You do not have to take it on faith. The wave's electric field flips the molecule's electrons back and forth, and a shorter wave flips them faster. An accelerating charge radiates, and the power it radiates grows as the fourth power of how fast it is being shaken. Frequency is just wavelength turned inside out, so faster shaking means shorter wavelength, and the fourth power lands on wavelength.

Lord Rayleigh published this in the 1870s, before anyone knew what an electron was. He got the right answer from the geometry of the situation, and the electron only confirmed it later.

Four things this one rule explains
  • The sky is blue. Short waves are scattered out of the beam and arrive at you from every direction at once.
  • Sunsets are red. Over a long path, those same short waves are removed from the direct beam and never reach you.
  • Distant mountains look bluish. More air between you and the ridge means more scattered blue laid over it, which is why painters push far hills toward blue.
  • The sky is even brighter in ultraviolet. UV is shorter than violet, so it scatters harder still. You can be sunburned in open shade by scattered light with the Sun nowhere in view.
Chapter 2 · Step 3

Look away from the Sun and it still glows

Pick any patch of sky far from the Sun. Light is reaching you from there. It did not travel in a straight line from the Sun to your eye, because the Sun is not in that direction.

It got there by turning a corner. Sunlight entered the atmosphere, hit air molecules, and headed off toward you. Since blue turns corners far more easily, the light arriving from all over the sky is loaded with blue. The entire dome appears to glow.

One myth to retire: the sky is not blue because it reflects the ocean. A planet of nothing but brown desert would still have a blue sky.

Chapter 2 · Step 4

So why is it not violet?

Here is the puzzle. Violet has a shorter wavelength than blue, so 1/λ⁴ says violet should win. Turn on only the first toggle and the model agrees: it predicts a violet sky.

Now add the second effect. Sunlight does not arrive with equal amounts of every color. There is less usable light at the far violet edge than across the blue and green.

Then add the third. Your are far less sensitive at the deep violet edge than in the blue and blue green. Even when violet light is present, it produces a weak signal.

Stack all three and the swatch lands on the color you have seen your whole life. The sky's color is a fact about physics and a fact about the eye reading it.

absorption also shapes the spectrum, and it matters a great deal during some twilights. Rayleigh scattering is still the central explanation for the ordinary blue daytime sky, and researchers are still refining the details.

How a blue sky becomes a red sunset

Chapter 3 · Step 3

So where did the blue go?

It did not vanish. It was scattered off in other directions, which means somebody, somewhere, is receiving it. Often that somebody is you, looking at a different part of the sky.

That is why a good sunset has layers: deep blue high overhead, a pale washed band near the horizon, and burning orange right around the Sun. You are looking at in one direction and scattered light in another.

Why clouds catch fire. Cloud droplets are enormous compared with gas molecules, so they scatter all visible colors nearly equally and normally look white or gray. At sunset the light hitting them has already had its blue stripped out. The clouds faithfully bounce whatever they are given, and they are being given orange.

Clouds are screens. The filtering happened before the light ever got to them.

Sunrise and sunset run on identical physics. The differences you notice come from cloud cover, humidity, smoke, dust and wind. A red sunset is not a measurement of any one substance, which is why "red sky at night" is a weak forecast and a good poem.

Chapter 4 Night, dust and shadow

Red moons, smoke and the other kind of scattering

The same rule that paints the sky also paints the Moon. Then particles show up that are too big to obey it.

Chapter 4 · Step 1

A low Moon is an orange Moon

The Moon makes no light of its own. Moonlight is sunlight that bounced off gray lunar rock. So moonlight has to cross our atmosphere just like sunlight does, and it pays the same toll.

High overhead, short path, pale gray white. Down near the horizon, long path, blue stripped away, and the Moon turns butter yellow, then orange, then a deep smoky red. Dust and pollution push it further.

The Moon has not changed. The change happens in the last hundred kilometers of the trip.

Chapter 4 · Step 2

The eclipse Moon is a different trick

A total lunar eclipse looks similar and works differently. Drag the Moon into Earth's shadow and follow the six steps.

Earth slides between the Sun and the Moon and blocks the direct sunlight. But some sunlight grazes the outer edge of our atmosphere. Blue gets scattered away hard. The atmosphere then , meaning bends, the surviving orange and red light inward, into the , the deep part of Earth's shadow. That reddened light lands on the Moon and reflects back to you.

NASA's way of putting it is worth memorizing: you are seeing the light of every sunrise and every sunset on Earth, all at once, projected onto the Moon.

Eclipse color varies from bright copper to a nearly black brownish red, depending on clouds, dust and volcanic particles circling our planet that night.

Why you cannot get one every month

You need two things at once and they run on different clocks. The Moon has to be at full, meaning on the far side of Earth from the Sun, and it has to be crossing the flat plane that Earth orbits in. Its own orbit is tilted about five degrees, so most full Moons pass above or below the shadow and nothing happens. Set the tilt to zero and you will get an eclipse every single month, which is a useful way to see that the tilt is the entire reason they are rare.

Sizes and distances here are compressed hard so everything fits. In reality Earth is 150 million km from the Sun and the Moon is 384,000 km from Earth, which is about a quarter of one percent as far. Earth's shadow at the Moon's distance is roughly 2.6 times wider than the Moon, and the Moon crawls through it over several hours.

Chapter 4 · Step 3

When the particles get too big for Rayleigh

Rayleigh's rule holds when the scatterer is much smaller than the wavelength. Nitrogen and oxygen qualify easily. often do not.

Aerosols are tiny solid bits or liquid droplets floating in air: mineral dust, wildfire smoke, soot, sea salt, sulfate droplets, pollen, industrial pollution.

When a particle is about the same size as a wavelength or bigger, we describe it with , named for physicist Gustav Mie. Slide the particle radius up and watch the pattern change shape.

Two honest footnotes. Mie's original solution is for perfect spheres, and real soot and dust are lumpy, so researchers use heavier models. And "Mie scattering" has become a general classroom label for scattering by anything comparable to or larger than a wavelength.

Mie type scattering treats visible colors more evenly, which is why haze reads as white, pale gray or dirty yellow rather than blue. It also kills contrast, throws glare around the Sun and washes out the horizon.

Chapter 4 · Step 4

Does pollution make better sunsets?

Sometimes. Not always. Run the mixer and find the sweet spot yourself.

A moderate aerosol load scatters and absorbs extra blue and green, which can deepen the Sun into a stronger red. Iron bearing dust absorbs some short wavelengths. Smoke can carry both scattering organic particles and strongly absorbing black carbon.

Push the load higher and the sky gives up. A thick layer dims the Sun, hides the clouds that would have caught the color, and flattens everything to gray or brown. More is not better, and past a point more is much worse.

What decides the outcome: particle size, concentration, chemistry, altitude, humidity, your viewing direction, where the clouds are, and whether the particles mostly scatter or mostly absorb.

Nature also refuses to sort itself neatly. Very fine smoke can behave close to Rayleigh, while larger droplets and dust need Mie or heavier models. The two categories are a teaching tool, not a fence.

Chapter 5 · Step 1

Why a glass of milky water is a real atmosphere

Everything in this lesson has been about one situation: light travelling a long way through a medium full of particles much smaller than it is. Nothing about that requires a planet. It requires a path, a light source, and scatterers of the right size. A tank of water with a little milk in it supplies all three, which is why this demonstration has been in atmospheric science courses for over a century.

Milk is water with fat globules and casein protein clusters suspended in it. Diluted hard, those particles are small enough to scatter short wavelengths preferentially, exactly the way air molecules do. Concentrated milk is white for the same reason clouds are: the particles are effectively large and scatter everything evenly.

The single most important instruction is to look twice. Look at the tank from the side, then walk around and look down the beam from the far end. Same light. Same instant. Two different colors. Almost nobody expects that the first time, and once you have seen it, the sky stops being mysterious.

Light seen from the side can be a completely different color from light that made it all the way through.

Chapter 5 · Step 2

Now run it on a table

The screen version cannot be argued with, and that is exactly its weakness. Do it for real and you get to be surprised, which is worth more than being told.

What you need. A clear rectangular container. A rectangular vase, a small aquarium or a clear plastic shoebox all work, and a tall drinking glass will do if that is what you have. About two liters of water. A white LED flashlight, the brighter the better, or a phone light. Whole milk. A dropper, a straw or a clean spoon. A sheet of white paper. A dark room.

Setup. Fill the container with water. Stand the paper up behind the far end. Put the flashlight flat against the near end so the beam runs the long way through the water, not down through the top. Turn the room lights off and let your eyes adjust for a minute, because the side glow is faint at first and bright room lights will hide it completely.

Doses. This is where most attempts fail. People add far too much milk. In two liters of water you want roughly a quarter teaspoon of whole milk in total, arrived at a few drops at a time. If the water goes cloudy white immediately, you overshot. Pour half out, top it up with clean water and carry on.

Procedure. Add two or three drops and stir gently. Look from the side. Then move your head so you are looking straight down the beam at the paper. Write down both colors. Add two more drops and repeat. Keep going until the far end is clearly orange or red. Then add several more and watch the effect die as the tank turns white and opaque, which is worth seeing on purpose because it is the same thing that happens to a sunset under heavy smoke.

Predict before every addition. Say out loud what the paper spot will look like after the next drops before you add them. Being wrong out loud is how this sticks.

If it is not working. Room too bright is the usual cause. After that: too much milk, a beam that is too short or aimed through the shallow direction of the container, or a warm yellow incandescent bulb instead of a white LED, which starts you so far toward orange that you cannot see the shift.

Push it further. Try skim milk against whole milk, since the fat content changes the particle sizes. Look at the beam through a polarizing filter or sunglasses and rotate them, because scattered light is partly polarized and the side glow will dim and brighten, which is the same effect that makes a polarizer darken a blue sky. Compare a few drops of milk with a pinch of flour, where the particles are far too big and the whole thing just goes white.

The honest limitation. Milk droplets are far larger and far more complicated than nitrogen and oxygen molecules. This is closer to an aerosol or Mie model than to true Rayleigh scattering, which is why the side glow is a pale washed blue rather than a deep sky blue. It still demonstrates the core idea, which is why every atmospheric science course still runs it.

Safety. Never look directly at the Sun. Do not substitute a laser for the flashlight. Keep the flashlight and any cords away from spilled water. Do not drink the mixture.

Twelve things worth keeping

Cover the list, say them out loud, then check

  1. Sunlight is a continuous mixture of visible wavelengths, not a single color.
  2. Nitrogen and oxygen do nearly all molecular scattering, because they are about 99 percent of dry air.
  3. An incoming light wave drives a molecule's electrons, and the molecule reradiates light in new directions at nearly the same wavelength.
  4. Rayleigh scattering is far stronger for short wavelengths, roughly as 1/λ⁴.
  5. Scattered blue light arriving from all over the sky is what makes the daytime dome look blue.
  6. The sky is not violet because of the solar spectrum, atmospheric transmission, and the wavelength sensitivity of human vision.
  7. At sunrise and sunset, light travels through far more atmosphere, measured as air mass.
  8. Short wavelengths are removed from the direct beam, producing atmospheric reddening. Nothing red is added.
  9. A low Moon reddens for exactly the same reason a low Sun does.
  10. A lunar eclipse is red because Earth's atmosphere filters sunlight and refracts the survivors into Earth's shadow.
  11. Dust, smoke and pollution aerosols both scatter and absorb light.
  12. Mie scattering usually produces white or gray haze, though some aerosols deepen red, orange and brown.
