Why the drills work this way

Every mode here is built on findings from memory research and on how actors actually get off book. Short version: reading the script again does very little, and struggling to produce the line does a great deal.

Retrieval beats rereading

Trying to recall something, and failing, then seeing the answer, builds a stronger memory than reading the same passage several more times. That is why every card makes you attempt the line before it shows you anything.

Spacing beats cramming

The same amount of practice spread over days holds far better than the same practice in one sitting. Each card carries its own schedule: a line you nail moves out to a longer gap, a line you fumble comes back within minutes. Grade yourself honestly and the schedule does the planning.

Cues are half the job

On stage you never recall a line from nothing. You recall it off the line before it. Every card leads with the cue and names who speaks it, so what you rehearse is the link, not the speech in isolation. Turn on Cue aloud and the app reads it to you, which is the old cue tape trick without a scene partner.

Fading support

The ladder runs first letters, to a few words dropped, to most words dropped, to nothing but the cue. Removing support gradually keeps you succeeding while the crutch shrinks. Adaptive mode moves each card along this ladder on its own as your grades improve.

First letters

An old technique for verse and speeches: reduce every word to its opening letter. Enough to catch you if you stumble, not enough to read from. Good for the last pass before you drop the book.

Order and structure

Most dry ups are not lost words, they are a lost sequence: right thoughts, wrong order. Put lines in order drills the shape of a speech rather than its wording.

Chunks, not walls

Long speeches are split into short passages of four lines or fewer so each one fits in working memory. The chunk boundaries follow the speech, and the tail of the previous chunk becomes the cue for the next.

Mix it up

Drilling one scene over and over feels productive and tests poorly. New passages are introduced in script order, so you learn the play forwards, but everything due for review is pulled from wherever it lives and shuffled through the session. Each sitting is a mix of new ground and old, which is harder in the moment and holds better later.

Out loud, on your feet

Speaking the line trains the muscle memory of breath and consonants, which silent reading does not. Say every answer aloud, and once you can, pair the words with the moves.

What the numbers mean

Each passage sits in one of seven boxes. A new passage is box zero. Grading Got it moves it up one, Solid moves it two, Shaky holds it, Lost it drops it to zero. Learned percentage is how far your passages have climbed, so it rises slowly at first and does not hit 100 until every passage has survived a long gap.

Experience is a nudge, not a score. You earn it for attempts, and more for clean ones, so a long careful session outranks a short lucky one.
